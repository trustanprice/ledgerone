# Credit Risk module

A self-contained practice module for vintage analysis and roll-rate /
transition-matrix SQL — built as interview prep for credit-risk /
loss-forecasting analyst roles (Synchrony-style: production reserve
development under CECL, roll-rate modeling, SR 11-7 / CCAR / DFAST model
governance context). Not a continuation of the main README's numbered
roadmap — Phase 3 (BI dashboards) and Phase 4 (investment simulation) stay
exactly as documented, untouched by this module.

Read this end to end before the interview — it's written to double as a
review sheet, not just documentation.

## Why this module exists

The e-commerce ledger (Phases 1-2 of the main project) is a general
event-sourced-ledger + analytics-engineering exercise. It doesn't touch the
two techniques that actually show up in credit-risk-analyst interviews and
take-home tests: **vintage analysis** and **roll-rate / transition-matrix
analysis**. This module adds a second, separate synthetic domain — a
consumer credit/loan portfolio — purely to get direct, repeatable SQL reps
on those two techniques, plus a simplified illustration of how a
transition-matrix roll-forward turns into a CECL reserve number.

## The synthetic data

Generated by `src/generate_credit_risk_data.py` (deterministic, `SEED = 42`,
same pattern as `src/generate_data.py`), written to
`data/raw/credit_risk/*.parquet`.

**`loan_originations`** — one row per account: `account_id`,
`origination_date`, `origination_score_band` (Subprime / Near Prime / Prime
/ Super Prime), `credit_limit`, `apr`, `product_type` (Private Label Card /
Dual Card / General Purpose Card — weighted toward private label, since
that's the actual mix of a Synchrony-style card issuer). Originations are
spread across 12 quarters (2022-Q1 through 2024-Q4) so vintage curves have
enough cohorts to compare.

**`loan_performance`** — one row per account per active month:
`account_id`, `performance_month`, `months_on_book`, `statement_balance`,
`payment_amount`, `delinquency_bucket` (Current / 30-59 DPD / 60-89 DPD /
90-119 DPD / 120+/Charged-Off), `charge_off_flag`.

### How the delinquency bucket evolves (the part worth understanding)

Each account's bucket is a **Markov chain**: next month's bucket depends
only on this month's bucket, sampled from a fixed transition-probability
table (`BASE_TRANSITIONS` in the generator). Roughly:

- **Current**: 99.4% stays Current, 0.6% rolls to 30-59 DPD (scaled by a
  per-band risk multiplier — Subprime ×2.5, Near Prime ×1.6, Prime ×1.0,
  Super Prime ×0.6, capped at 5%/month — so worse-underwritten accounts
  enter delinquency more often, which is the one place segment risk is
  modeled explicitly; cure/roll rates *within* delinquency don't vary by
  band, to keep the generator readable).
- **30-59 DPD**: 40% cures straight to Current, 20% stays, 40% rolls to
  60-89.
- **60-89 DPD**: 30% cures back one level to 30-59, 25% stays, 45% rolls to
  90-119.
- **90-119 DPD**: 20% cures back to 60-89, 20% stays, 60% rolls to
  120+/Charged-Off.
- **120+/Charged-Off** is **absorbing** — the generator emits exactly one
  row for the month an account charges off and then stops emitting rows
  for that account entirely (it exits the active book, the same way a
  charged-off account exits a real servicing population).

`statement_balance` and `payment_amount` are descriptive only — they revolve
and pay down when Current, grow with unpaid interest/fees when delinquent —
but they do **not** drive the bucket transition. That's a deliberate
simplification: keeping the Markov chain as the sole transition driver
keeps the resulting roll-rate structure clean and exactly queryable,
instead of being entangled with a separate balance-based cure/default rule.

Result, on a representative generated run: **5.05% blended lifetime
charge-off rate**, ranging from 2.0% (Super Prime) to 9.6% (Subprime) — a
realistic, monotonic risk gradient for unsecured revolving consumer credit.

## What each mart answers

All models are prefixed `credit_risk__` and live in their own dbt subfolder
(`dbt/models/credit_risk/`), kept fully separate from the e-commerce ledger
domain — no model joins across the two.

| Model | Grain | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| `credit_risk__stg_loan_originations` | 1 row / account | Typed, 1:1 staging of raw originations |
| `credit_risk__stg_loan_performance` | 1 row / account / month | Typed, 1:1 staging of raw performance |
| `credit_risk__vintage_curves` | 1 row / (origination_quarter, months_on_book) | How is this cohort performing at month N, vs. prior cohorts at the same age? |
| `credit_risk__roll_rate_transition_matrix` | 1 row / (transition_month, from_bucket, to_bucket) | What share of accounts in bucket X moved to bucket Y next month? |
| `credit_risk__cecl_reserve_estimate` | 1 row / current delinquency bucket | Given the book today, what's the 12-month expected loss and implied reserve rate? |
| `credit_risk__portfolio_summary_by_score_band` | 1 row / origination_score_band | How does composition/performance vary by underwriting segment? |

### Vintage curves — the technique

For each origination cohort (quarter) and months-on-book, what **cumulative**
share of the cohort has *ever* reached 30+/60+/90+ DPD or charged off by
that age? Built with a window `MIN()` to find the first month each account
crosses each severity threshold, then a running `SUM() OVER (PARTITION BY
cohort ORDER BY months_on_book ROWS UNBOUNDED PRECEDING)` to accumulate.
This is the standard first move in vintage analysis: it lets you compare
"is the 2024-Q2 cohort performing worse than 2023-Q2 was at the same age,"
which raw monthly snapshots can't answer directly (different cohorts are at
different points in their life at any given calendar month).

**Read it carefully**: later cohorts only have a few months of history as
of the data's observation window, so their curves are short — that's
normal right-censoring, not missing data. Always compare cohorts at equal
months-on-book, never at equal calendar date.

### Roll-rate transition matrix — the highest-value exercise here

The core SQL pattern: `LAG(delinquency_bucket) OVER (PARTITION BY account_id
ORDER BY months_on_book)` to get each account's *prior* bucket, pair it
with this month's bucket, `COUNT(*) GROUP BY (transition_month, from_bucket,
to_bucket)`, then row-normalize by dividing by `SUM(...) OVER (PARTITION BY
transition_month, from_bucket)` so each `from_bucket` row sums to 1. That
row-normalized table **is** the transition matrix — this exact pattern
(LAG + self-comparison + row-normalize) is what shows up in loss-forecasting
take-home tests, so it's worth being able to write cold.

### CECL reserve estimate — how roll-rate becomes a loss number

This is the piece most worth being able to explain out loud in an
interview. In plain terms:

1. Take the transition matrix above, blended across the whole observation
   window (not month-specific) — this is "how accounts in each bucket
   typically behave, on average."
2. Explicitly add a self-loop for 120+/Charged-Off → itself at probability
   1.0. It's a terminal state that's *never observed* as a `from_bucket` in
   the data (no rows exist after charge-off, by construction) — without
   adding this row, the roll-forward would silently lose probability mass
   every time it reached charge-off, which would understate the loss
   forecast. This is exactly the kind of absorbing-state bookkeeping a real
   Markov roll-rate model has to get right.
3. **Roll the matrix forward N months** (a dbt var, `cecl_forecast_horizon_months`,
   default 12) via a `WITH RECURSIVE` CTE: starting from each possible
   bucket with probability 1.0 in that bucket and 0 elsewhere, repeatedly
   multiply the current probability distribution by the transition matrix
   — `probability(state at month m+1) = Σ probability(prior state at month m) × rate(prior → state)`.
   After N iterations, the probability mass sitting in
   120+/Charged-Off is that starting bucket's **probability of charge-off
   within the horizon** — a lifetime-PD-like number, one per starting
   bucket.
4. Join that back to **today's book** (each account's latest snapshot,
   excluding accounts already charged off) and multiply:
   `expected_loss = outstanding_balance × prob_charge_off_within_horizon`.
   Sum by current bucket to get a reserve schedule; `reserve_rate =
   expected_loss / balance`.

On a representative run this produces a clean, monotonic result — a
current-bucket account has roughly a 1.9% chance of charging off within 12
months, rising to ~33% at 30-59 DPD, ~67% at 60-89, ~91% at 90-119 — and a
**2.97% blended reserve rate** against the whole outstanding book. That
shape (most of the reserve concentrated in the already-delinquent accounts,
not the current book) is exactly what real CECL reserve schedules look
like.

**What a real production model adds on top of this** (worth naming
explicitly, since interviewers will probe the gap): segmentation of the
transition matrix by risk band/vintage/product instead of one blended
matrix; macroeconomic overlays / forward-looking adjustments (CECL requires
"reasonable and supportable" forecasts, not just historical averages);
discounting future cash flows; qualitative overlays for factors the model
can't see; and a full model governance/validation process (SR 11-7:
independent validation, ongoing monitoring, documented limitations) before
a number like this could actually be booked as a reserve. This module
deliberately skips all of that to isolate and practice the one core
technique — say so if asked.

### A real gotcha worth knowing (DuckDB numeric literal typing)

Building `credit_risk__cecl_reserve_estimate`, the first version of the
recursive roll-forward silently produced garbage — clean-looking but wrong
values like exactly `0.1`, `0.4`, `0.8`. Cause: the recursive CTE's anchor
term wrote a bare `1.0` literal for the starting probability. DuckDB
inferred that literal as `DECIMAL(2,1)` (one digit of precision after the
decimal point) instead of `DOUBLE`, and every subsequent multiplication in
the recursion inherited that type, destroying precision at every step. Fix:
`CAST(1.0 AS DOUBLE)` on both literals. Worth remembering — implicit
numeric literal typing is a real, easy-to-miss source of silently wrong
aggregate/forecast numbers, and it's the kind of thing worth mentioning if
you're ever asked "tell me about a bug you found and how you found it."

## Tests

Every staging model and mart has schema tests (`not_null`, `unique`,
`accepted_values` on every categorical field, `relationships` on join
keys). Composite-grain models (`credit_risk__stg_loan_performance`,
`credit_risk__vintage_curves`, `credit_risk__roll_rate_transition_matrix`)
carry a deterministic surrogate key (`{{ dbt.hash(...) }}`, the same
pattern the e-commerce ledger's `stg_ledger_entries` already uses) so they
can take a standard `unique`/`not_null` test without a dbt_utils
dependency. One custom singular test,
`assert_roll_rate_transition_rates_sum_to_one`, checks that every
`(transition_month, from_bucket)` group in the roll-rate matrix actually
sums to 1 — a real invariant, not just a schema shape check.

## Validation: backtesting the methodology against real mortgage data

Everything above proves the *mechanics* work — correct SQL, passing
tests, internally consistent numbers, all on synthetic data hand-tuned to
look realistic. That's not evidence the **methodology** (vintage curves,
roll-rate transition matrices) actually forecasts real credit
performance. `notebooks/03_credit_risk_validation_backtest.ipynb` closes
that gap: it runs the same techniques against a real, public
loan-performance panel with a genuine time-based holdout, and reports how
accurate the forecasts actually were.

### Data source: Freddie Mac Single-Family Loan-Level Dataset

**Mortgage data, not credit cards** — a real, deliberate scope choice.
The alternative considered was Lending Club's historical accepted-loans
data (public on Kaggle, no registration beyond a Kaggle account), which
gives `loan_status` snapshots rather than a full monthly delinquency
panel — the roll-rate backtest would have been an approximation there
(no true month-over-month transition to compare), not the real thing.
Freddie Mac's dataset gives a genuine monthly panel, at the cost of a
one-time manual registration step (below). Since it's a different product
type, **loss levels won't match** Synchrony's card portfolio — what's
being validated is whether the vintage/roll-rate *methodology* forecasts
accurately on a real monthly loan-performance panel, which is the part
that transfers.

Specifically: the **2021 vintage sample** (a Freddie Mac-provided random
sample of 50,000 loans originated in 2021, with performance data through
March 2026 — about 5 years of history, giving genuine room for a
time-based holdout).

**How to reproduce this** (manual — Freddie Mac requires registration,
this can't be scripted):

1. Register for free at Freddie Mac's **Clarity Data Intelligence**
   portal (the Single-Family Loan-Level Dataset access point).
2. From the SFLLD Data Download page, download the **2021 sample**
   (`sample_2021.zip` under the "Sample" dataset — 50,000 loans, one
   origination file + one monthly performance file).
3. Unzip and place the two `.txt` files at:
   `data/external/freddie_mac/sample_orig_2021.txt` and
   `data/external/freddie_mac/sample_perf_2021.txt` (gitignored — not
   redistributed in this repo; see `data/AGENTS.md`).
4. Run `python src/ingest_freddie_mac_validation_data.py` to parse and
   map them, then open and run
   `notebooks/03_credit_risk_validation_backtest.ipynb`.

**Column layout was verified, not assumed.** Freddie Mac has revised
their published file layout multiple times since 2018 (from 27 columns
up to 32 today). Before writing the ingestion script, the *current*
(January 2026) General User Guide was fetched directly from
freddiemac.com and cross-checked against the actual sample files by
**value distribution**, not just documented position — e.g. the loan
identifier column was confirmed as such because every value is a unique
`PYYQnXXXXXX`-shaped string across all 50,000 rows, not because a PDF
said "column 20." Two real findings fell out of that verification: the
origination file has one fewer field than the current doc (missing the
newest field, consistent with predating its addition), and the
performance file has three *more* fields than documented (undocumented,
unused, not guessed at). See
`src/ingest_freddie_mac_validation_data.py`'s docstring for the full
column-by-column mapping.

### Bucket mapping: a modeling decision, not a mechanical translation

Freddie Mac's delinquency status is a 2-digit MBA-method code (`'00'`
current, `'01'` = 30-59 days, `'02'` = 60-89, `'03'` = 90-119, higher =
more months delinquent, `'RA'` = REO acquisition). Mapped onto this
project's existing 5-bucket scheme:

| Freddie Mac code | This project's bucket |
|---|---|
| `'00'` | Current |
| `'01'` | 30-59 DPD |
| `'02'` | 60-89 DPD |
| `'03'` | 90-119 DPD |
| `'04'`+ or `'RA'` | 120+/Charged-Off |

**Mortgages don't "charge off" the way credit cards do** — there's no
single event that zeroes the balance the way a card issuer's charge-off
does. `120+/Charged-Off` here is used as a **serious-delinquency /
foreclosure-adjacent loss proxy**, explicitly labeled as such throughout
the notebook — a real, stated difference from the synthetic card module
this backtest validates against, not glossed over.

### Why a time-based split, not a random one

A random split would let the model see performance from later in a
loan's life while "training" on data that chronologically includes
information from after the points it's being tested against — leakage.
Fit the transition matrix on data through a cutoff month, forecast
forward, compare against what actually happened after that cutoff: that's
the only split that honestly answers "if this model had been built on
what was known as of the cutoff, how well would it have forecast what
actually happened next?" — the real question a production backtest has
to answer. The notebook trains through **September 2024** and holds out
the trailing **18 months** (through March 2026) per cohort.

### Headline results

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| MAE (cumulative 90+ DPD rate, holdout) | **0.19 percentage points** |
| MAPE (same, where actual > 0) | 12.5% — noisy at these small base rates (~1-2%), see notebook §6 |
| Mean bias | **+0.19pp** (the model slightly *over*-predicts) |
| Comparison points | 90 (5 cohorts × up to 18 holdout months) |
| This vintage's lifetime ever-90+ DPD rate | 2.01% |
| FRED credit card delinquency rate, Q1 2026 (DRCCLACBS) | 2.90% |
| Synchrony disclosed net charge-off rate, Q1 2026 | 5.42% |

**The single most useful finding** is in the roll-rate calibration table
(notebook §7), not the headline MAE: the transition matrix isn't
time-stable. Cure rates from early-stage delinquency (30-59 DPD back to
Current) measurably worsened between the training window (2021-2024) and
the holdout window (late 2024-2026) — a real macro/rate-environment shift
a single blended, time-invariant matrix has no way to anticipate. That's
the concrete, textbook reason production CECL models carry macro
overlays instead of extrapolating one historical matrix forward
indefinitely.

**Plain-language read**: the vintage-curve / roll-rate methodology
transfers reasonably well from synthetic data to a real mortgage
panel — the cumulative forecast lands within ~0.2 percentage points of
reality, and the mechanics hold up end to end on a real, messy dataset.
The real, honest gap is that a static matrix drifts out of calibration
as conditions change, which the holdout period demonstrates directly
rather than just asserting. See the notebook's final section for the
full discussion, including a real bug caught and fixed mid-analysis
(the first draft's "already reached serious delinquency" base rate used
each account's *current* bucket at the cutoff snapshot instead of
whether it had *ever* reached serious delinquency — silently
undercounting accounts that cured before the snapshot, and producing a
misleading bias that had nothing to do with the model itself).

## Running it

Part of the same local dbt + DuckDB + Parquet stack as the rest of the
project — no new tooling. `make all` (or `make generate` /
`make build`) picks this domain up automatically; there's no separate
command. To build/test just this module:

```
export LEDGERONE_DB_PATH=.../data/processed/ledgerone.duckdb
export LEDGERONE_RAW_DIR=.../data/raw
cd dbt
dbt build --profiles-dir . --select credit_risk
```

To eyeball the vintage curves and transition matrix, open
`notebooks/02_credit_risk_vintage_and_roll_rates.ipynb` (read-only, plots
only — no dashboard app, per this module's scope).
